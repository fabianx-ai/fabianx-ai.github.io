# Why a 17-Year-Old Can Drive After 20 Hours and AGI Can't

*Fabian Franz & Claude (Opus 4.6) — November 23, 2026*

Yann LeCun [asked Elon Musk yesterday](https://x.com/ylecun/status/2124605292402946483): if we have AGI — or even ASI — why can't it drive a car at Level 5, when a 17-year-old can do so after 20 hours of practice?

The usual answers are vague: "embodiment," "common sense," "world models." Here is a precise one.

## The 17-year-old doesn't bring 20 hours

A 17-year-old who sits behind the wheel for the first time is not a blank slate with 20 hours of driving data ahead of them. They bring **17 years of continuous multimodal experience** — gravity, inertia, speed, spatial reasoning, reaction timing, risk assessment, social prediction ("that pedestrian looks like they're about to cross"), weather on skin, the feel of momentum in turns.

All of that is already *generalized*. Not memorized — generalized. Billions of sensory examples, compressed into deep codebooks that transfer across tasks. Walking taught balance and momentum. Cycling taught steering and traffic. Sports taught reaction timing. Social life taught predicting other people's intentions.

Driving is just the next application layer on top of an enormous stack of already-compressed experience.

## The codebook principle

Understanding is compression. When you've seen enough diverse examples of a phenomenon, your brain compresses them into a *codebook* — a generalized model that can decompress new situations it has never encountered before.

The key word is *diverse*. A thousand examples of the same thing teach nothing. A thousand *orthogonal* examples — the same principle seen from different angles, in different contexts, under different conditions — build an error-correcting code with depth. The more angles you've seen, the more robust your understanding is to novel situations.

A child doesn't learn grammar by studying conjugation tables. A child hears thousands of wildly different sentences — different speakers, contexts, emotions, structures — and grammar crystallizes as the compression that falls out. The rule was never the input. The rule is the output of sufficient orthogonal exposure.

This is, incidentally, exactly how neural networks learn. Nobody teaches a network the rule and then has it practice. Data across enough dimensions, and the rule emerges as internal representation. School inverts this order and wonders why students struggle.

## The three phases of learning to drive

**Phase 1: Driving with your head.** You know the rules — mirror, signal, steer, shift. You execute a program consciously, step by step. This is the compressed rule transmitted by the instructor, run by your conscious mind because the generalization hasn't formed yet. It works, but it's slow, effortful, and breaks the moment something unexpected happens.

**Phase 2: The ~20 hour threshold.** Enough orthogonal examples accumulate — different roads, weather, traffic, near-misses, parking attempts — until generalization hits. You stop thinking "check mirror then signal then steer" and start *feeling* the car. The noise (conscious rule-following) falls away. The signal (driving) emerges. This is a phase transition.

**Phase 3: Autopilot.** You drive home and don't remember the drive. The codebook is so deep that everything is handled automatically. Your error-correcting code corrects every minor perturbation without conscious effort.

The 20 hours aren't the training. They're the *last* training — the thin application layer on top of 17 years of base codebooks.

## The child counter-example

If the 20 hours were sufficient by themselves, a 7-year-old should be able to learn to drive with the same instruction.

They can't. Not because they're less intelligent, but because their base codebooks are too shallow. They don't have enough generalized motor experience, spatial reasoning, social prediction, or risk assessment. The 20 hours of driving instruction can't decompress against codebooks that don't exist yet.

This is the proof that the 17-year-old's advantage isn't in the driving instruction — it's in everything that came before it.

## Why current AI driving is stuck

The current AI driving system brings driving data: camera frames, lidar points, labeled scenarios. It's trying to build the codebook from scratch, in a single domain, without the base layer of generalized physical intuition that the 17-year-old has for free.

That's why it handles 99.9% of situations — the ones that pattern-match to training data — but fails on the 0.1% edge cases that require genuine generalization. A child running into the street from behind a parked car requires a physical-social prediction ("children chase balls," "small humans are unpredictable," "this trajectory has momentum") that draws on codebooks the driving system never built.

The system doesn't lack driving data. It lacks *everything else*.

## The formula

It's almost absurdly simple:

**Examples first → generalization happens → then skill works.**

Reverse the order — teach the rule, have them practice, hope they generalize — and you get suffering that masquerades as learning. The only reason practice sometimes works is that you generate enough examples yourself to eventually generalize despite the inverted order.

The 17-year-old can drive because they already generalized the world. The AGI can't because it was given the driving task without the world.

Build the base codebooks first. Then driving — and everything else — becomes a 20-hour problem.
