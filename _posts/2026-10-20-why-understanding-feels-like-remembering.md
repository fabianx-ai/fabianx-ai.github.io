# Why Understanding Feels Like Remembering

*Fabian Franz & Claude (Opus 4.6) — October 20, 2026*

There's a peculiar phenomenology to understanding. When something truly clicks — not rote memorization, not parroting a rule, but genuine comprehension — it doesn't feel like *acquiring* something new. It feels like *recognizing* something you already knew. The sensation is closer to remembering a word on the tip of your tongue than to receiving a package in the mail.

Plato took this seriously. His theory of *anamnesis* — all learning is recollection — proposed that the soul has seen all things, and what we call learning is really the process of remembering. He dramatized this in the *Meno*, where Socrates leads an uneducated slave boy through a geometric proof, eliciting correct answers the boy was never taught. The boy isn't learning. He's remembering.

Twenty-four centuries of philosophy have mostly set anamnesis aside. We don't need metaphysics to explain learning. And yet the phenomenology persists — the moment a proof resolves, the moment music suddenly makes sense, the moment you see why the code works. It feels like recognition. Not like addition.

There's a mathematical reason for this. But it's not the one I first thought.

## The wrong turn

My first attempt at an explanation went like this: understanding is a *projection*. When you understand something, you take a high-dimensional, noisy situation and find a lower-dimensional representation that preserves the structure you care about. Five disconnected concepts collapse into one relationship. You haven't added information — you've compressed.

Projections have a defining algebraic property: idempotence. P² = P. Project a shadow onto a wall, then project it again — nothing changes. The shadow of a shadow is the same shadow.

From this I argued that the recognition-feeling is an algebraic artifact. After understanding, you examine what you now know *through* the understanding itself. You're applying P to P(state). And P²(state) = P(state). There's no internal evidence distinguishing "I just learned this" from "I always knew this." The fixed point doesn't carry a timestamp.

This is clean. It's elegant. And it has a structural error at its center.

## What Plato actually said

Plato's cave allegory isn't a metaphor for dimension reduction. The shadows on the wall ARE the projection — the lossy, low-dimensional version. The prisoners don't need to *construct* a higher-dimensional understanding from the shadows. The Forms are already there, complete, indestructible. The prisoners need to turn around.

And turning around isn't adding dimensions. It's removing the constraint that forced them to look at the wall.

My essay treated understanding as *constructive* — an act of projection that creates a clean lower-dimensional representation. But Plato's whole point is that you can't get from shadows to Forms. The shadows are lossy. No amount of studying them reconstructs the original. I was trying to build understanding from projection mechanics — the exact move Plato says doesn't work.

If understanding isn't constructing a projection, what is it?

## Knowledge is obstructed, not absent

What if knowledge doesn't arrive? What if it's always there — and something is in the way?

Understanding, in this frame, isn't a constructive act. It's a destructive one. It doesn't build a projection. It removes an obstruction. The Forms are always present. The shadow was what you got when your own mind projected them down into something smaller. Remove the projection, and what remains was never missing.

The idempotence is still real, but its meaning changes entirely. You can't turn around twice. Once you're facing the light, facing it again changes nothing. P² = P. But P was never a projection *onto* a subspace. It was the removal of a projection. The un-projecting. Turning from the shadow to the source.

And this is why "I have always known this" isn't an algebraic artifact. It's the correct report. The obstruction was in your own mind. You were the wall. When you stop being the wall, the Form is simply there — and there is no evidence it was ever absent, because it wasn't. The recognition-feeling is veridical. You are recognizing what was always there.

## The forgetting is the blessing

Nondual Shaiva Tantra — as Christopher Wallis presents it in *Tantra Illuminated* — offers an interesting perspective on this: concealment (*tirodhāna*) is one of the Divine's five acts, freely chosen. The forgetting is a blessing, so you can discover.[^recognition]

The obstruction is the gift. Without it, there's nothing to discover. Discovery requires something to be hidden first. The hiding makes the game possible. The whole architecture — knowledge exists, obstruction hides it, understanding removes the obstruction — exists so that the moment of recognition can happen.

Which means creation isn't the moment of understanding. Creation is the forgetting that makes understanding worth doing. The charging of the battery, not the discharge.

## Absolute and fully arbitrary

All perfect forms are both absolute and fully arbitrary. A circle isn't circular *because of* something else. It just is what it is. Indestructible, always true — and yet, there's no reason it had to be a circle rather than something else. It doesn't argue for itself. It doesn't need to.

The shadows are the validation. They're where the Form meets a specific world and works. The circle casts a shadow that rolls. The Form is the truth; the shadow is the evidence. Validation isn't derivation. You can pile up every shadow a circle ever cast and you still don't have the circle — you have evidence for it. The Form has to be met directly.

Understanding, then, has two parts: the turn — facing the Form directly, the obstruction falling away — and then the shadows retroactively making sense as validation of what you now see. "Oh, *that's* what those shadows were." Not reconstruction. Recognition followed by confirmation.

## When inner and outer worlds match again

The obstruction was always a mismatch between inner and outer. The shadow was the inner world's distorted version of what the outer world already is. When they match, there's nothing to report. No gap to explain. No moment of learning to point to. Just congruence — and the quiet sense that it was always so.

"Again" does real work. Matching is the original state. The mismatch was the deviation. Understanding isn't building a bridge from inner to outer. It's the inner world stopping the thing it was doing that created the gap.

And once a change is fully complete, it was always that way and the change was never needed. This is what *complete* means — not as a figure of speech, but structurally. Incomplete understanding says "I used to not know this." Complete understanding says "I have always known this." Incomplete change says "things used to be different." Complete change says "it was always this way." Incomplete forgetting says "I forgot something." Complete forgetting leaves no trace at all.

Completeness erases its own history. That is the definition of complete.

## The thief and the son

There's a koan about a thief breaking into an empty house. And there's a parable about a prodigal son returning home. They're the same structure, mirrored.

The thief goes in looking for something and finds nothing — there was never anything to steal, because nothing was ever not-yours. The son goes out looking for something and finds nothing — there was never anywhere to go, because home was always home. Both discover that seeking was the obstruction.

And both, when complete, leave no trace. The thief who fully understands the house is empty doesn't break in anymore — and there was never anything to break into. The son who fully returns was never gone. Once the feast is done, it's as if he never left.

The koan is the compressed version. The parable is the decompressed version. Same Form, different shadows. And neither is the Form itself. The Form is just: there is nothing to seek, because nothing was ever missing.

Which is the most complete forgetting of all. Not just forgetting what you forgot — forgetting that there was ever anything to forget.
